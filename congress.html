// Chapter 01 — The Setup

Two rooms,
one vibe shift.

Congress is a bicameral legislature — fancy word for "two chambers." Together they make federal law. Apart, they feel like two entirely different schools.

The House
435

More members. More chaos.

Reps serve 2-year terms. Every single one is up for re-election every other year, so they're in constant campaign mode. House = loud, fast, populist.

The Senate
100

Fewer members. More swag.

Two per state, no matter if it's Wyoming or California. 6-year terms mean senators can play the long game. Senate = slower, older, filibuster-heavy.

Who represents you?

Residents of each state have two U.S. senators and a House member for their congressional district. D.C. and U.S. territories have different representation and do not have voting senators. A ZIP code may overlap more than one House district.

// How legislation works

A bill’s path
is not a straight line.

Here is a common route. Bills may stall, be amended, or follow special procedures. Both chambers must pass identical text before presenting a bill to the president.

01 · Introduction

People and organizations can suggest policies. A member introduces a bill in Congress. Revenue-raising bills must originate in the House; the Senate may amend them.

02 · Committee

Committees specialize in topics, hold hearings, and may amend and report legislation. A bill can remain in committee without advancing.

03 · First chamber

Members debate and vote under their chamber’s rules. Ordinary passage generally needs a majority of those voting, with a quorum present.

04 · Other chamber

The other chamber considers the bill and may change it. The Senate’s cloture procedure for most legislation usually needs 60 votes when all 100 seats are filled; final passage is a separate vote.

05 · Agree on text

Both chambers must agree on the same version. They can exchange amendments or use a conference committee. A conference is not required for every bill.

06 · President

The president may sign or veto. After 10 days, excluding Sundays, without a signature, a bill becomes law unless Congress’s adjournment prevents its return; that exception is a pocket veto.

07 · After a veto

Congress may override with two-thirds in each chamber. The override is conditional, not a step every bill takes.

Committee vocabulary

Standing: continuing subject-matter work. Select/special: assigned a particular purpose; duration varies. Joint: members from both chambers. Conference: resolves differences on legislation. Some committees have distinctive powers.

// Article I

What Congress
can actually do.

The Constitution grants Congress specified powers and provides checks among the branches. A constitutional power is not a blank check; the way it is exercised can be challenged.

Taxes and spending

Congress can levy federal taxes, borrow, and authorize spending. State and local governments have their own tax powers; a sales tax at a local store is not generally a federal tax.

War and commerce

Congress can declare war and regulate commerce with foreign nations and among states. The president is commander in chief. Their roles can create difficult separation-of-powers questions.

Impeachment and oversight

The House impeaches; the Senate tries impeachments. Conviction requires two-thirds of senators present. Impeachment itself does not remove an official. Congress also conducts oversight and investigations.

Appointments and treaties

The Senate provides advice and consent on many presidential appointments. Treaty consent requires two-thirds of senators present; the president carries out ratification.

Constitutional amendments

Congress can propose an amendment with two-thirds in each chamber. Ratification by three-fourths of the states is then required. Article V also provides a convention route.

Necessary and proper

Congress may enact laws necessary and proper for carrying its constitutional powers into execution. This clause does not erase constitutional limits.

// Power and incentives

Follow the decision.
Ask who benefits.

Representation involves competing interests. Distinguish a legal process, a criticism of that process, and evidence of wrongdoing.

Gerrymandering

Drawing districts to advantage a political group can change how votes translate into seats. Unusual shapes alone do not establish unfairness.

Earmarks and “pork”

An earmark directs funds to a specified recipient or project. “Pork barrel” is a critical label for spending viewed as serving narrow political interests. Evaluate costs, beneficiaries, and alternatives before judging.

Lobbying

People and organizations try to influence officials. Lobbying is not inherently bribery; students, businesses, unions, and nonprofits may advocate for policy. Disclosure rules and access are important questions.

Logrolling

Members exchange support for one another’s proposals. Ask whether the compromise advances public goals or mainly protects insiders.

// Spending and accountability

Public investment
or political favor?

“Pork” is a judgment, not a neutral budget category. A small population or unusual project name does not settle whether spending is worthwhile. Investigate before deciding.

Define the project

What precisely is funded? Is the number a proposal, an appropriation, or actual spending? Over how many years?

Identify who benefits

Consider direct users, wider economic effects, public safety, and people outside the project’s location.

Compare alternatives

What would happen without the project? Are there less expensive ways to achieve the goal?

Look for accountability

Read the official project description, recipient, sponsor, selection process, and evaluation requirements.

// Influence and evidence

Who gets heard?

Lobbying is an attempt to influence government decisions. Campaign donations and lobbying are different activities with different rules. The question is not only whether advocacy is legal, but how access and accountability work.

Compare the arguments

A group may bring expertise and information officials need. Another concern is that organizations with more resources can gain greater access. What evidence would help evaluate each claim?

Look for disclosures

Federal lobbying rules require covered lobbyists and organizations to register and report certain activity. Read the disclosure framework ↗

PACs and Super PACs

Political action committees participate in campaign finance. Super PACs may raise unlimited contributions for independent political activity but cannot contribute directly to candidates. Check the FEC for the rules that apply to each committee type. FEC explanation ↗

Your voice

Advocacy is also a student explaining a transit problem or a community group asking for safer streets. Identify your issue, document the need, and make a specific request.

// Budget literacy

A deficit is a flow.
Debt is a stock.

A deficit is spending above revenue over a period, usually a fiscal year. Debt is accumulated borrowing outstanding at a point in time. They are related but not interchangeable.

Try a budget

The lab gives you 100 fictional revenue credits and five spending categories. Explain the tradeoffs in your choices. A balanced allocation is not automatically a good policy.

Read the real numbers

Use official data and record its date. Gross federal debt and debt held by the public are different measures, so label the one you use. Treasury finance guide ↗ · Debt to the Penny ↗
